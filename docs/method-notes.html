Massing Studio — Method Notes

What this tool computes, how it computes it, and — the part that matters most when you cite it — what it does not model at all. Everything below is read from the source code, not from what a plan-and-extrude tool is generally assumed to do. Where the code makes something unclear, this page says so rather than inferring.

Audience Architecture students and staff Method Plan drawing · straight extrusion · measurement · no analysis Runs Entirely in the browser Version 1.1.0 · 26 September 2026

Three things to know before you cite a number

  1. "GFA" here is a sum of footprints, not of floors. The status bar's GFA figure, and each level's area in the Levels panel, add up the plan area of every extruded shape once — whatever its height. A single volume drawn 9 m tall counts as one footprint, not three storeys; two shapes that overlap on the same level are both counted in full, overlap included. The figure is a true gross floor area only when every storey is drawn as its own shape, on its own level, with no overlaps — and nothing in the tool checks that it is.
  2. Snapping is stronger than the Snap checkbox, and weaker than the grid. Snapping to an existing shape's corner is checked first and fires whether or not Snap is on — when drawing, moving, scaling, editing a point, and placing a dimension. Turn Snap off and everything else goes free-hand, with nothing on screen marking a coordinate as off-grid. And even with Snap on, the 3D push/pull tool rounds a height to 0.1 m, not to the grid, unless it lands within 12 screen pixels of another volume's top or bottom or a level's elevation.
  3. Nothing here is analysis. The tool draws a plan, extrudes it by a height, measures lengths and areas, and exports the result. No module computes a structural, thermal, daylight or cost quantity of any kind, and there is no solver — iterative or otherwise — anywhere in the source.

The one formula worth naming

The area calculation is the shoelace formula, named as such in the source's own comment:

// Shoelace formula — signed area (positive = counter-clockwise in plan coords). sum = Σ (x_i × y_i+1 − x_i+1 × y_i) area = sum / 2

Since version 1.1.0 this number is shown to you — as a positive value, with any holes subtracted — in the Properties panel, the Levels panel and the status bar. It still also tells the push/pull tool which way an edge faces, and catches a drag that would collapse a footprint to nothing. Trap 1 is about what those displayed sums do and do not add up to.

02

The model and its conventions

Units

The project state carries a single units field, set once to 'm' when a new project is created. Nothing in the interface reads or changes it afterwards — there is no unit switcher, and the field is never referenced anywhere else in the source. Every number on screen is a metre, because the code never considers it could be anything else.

Coordinates, and which way is up

A shape's points are plan coordinates, x and y, on the page you draw on. Extruding maps plan x to world X, and the extrusion direction to world Y, which is "up" in the 3D view. Plan y becomes world Z, negated. A point drawn at plan (x, y) sits at 3D (x, b, −y), where b is its level's elevation plus the shape's own base offset, and rises to (x, b + height, −y) at its top. Every export and every .obj this tool opens uses the same convention: Y up, metres.

Levels as floors

A level carries a name, an elevation — the height its floor sits at — a default height that every new shape drawn on it receives, a colour, and visible and locked flags. All of them are edited in the Levels panel. + Add level places a new level at the top of the tallest existing one (its elevation plus its default height), named Floor 1, Floor 2 and so on, counting the Ground Floor as zero. Restack re-sets every elevation so each level sits exactly on the one below; Colour as gradient recolours every level, and every shape on it, from warm orange at the lowest to pale straw at the highest. Nothing checks that levels don't overlap, or that a volume stays within its level's height: a 9 m volume on a 3 m level simply passes through the two levels above it.

A shape also carries a base offset, in metres above its level's elevation, which is how one volume sits on top of another within a level. The Properties panel accepts any number for it, negative included; dragging a volume vertically in 3D (Alt+drag) stops at ground level. "Stack copy on top" duplicates a footprint with its underside at the original's top — onto the level whose elevation matches that height to the millimetre, if one exists, otherwise on the same level with a larger base offset.

The plan view shows the active level's shapes normally and every other level's at 35 % opacity. A click prefers a shape on the active level; selecting a shape on another level makes that level active.

Shape types

A rectangle, a polygon, a curve and a reference line all store an ordered list of points; for a curve those are the points you clicked, and the smooth outline is recomputed from them whenever it is needed. A circle stores a centre and a radius instead. Every shape also carries an id, its level, whether it is closed, a height, a base offset, a colour and an opacity, and a polygon made by a boolean operation can also carry holes. That is the complete set of fields a shape can hold. The points of a polygon, a curve or a line can be dragged one at a time; a rectangle or a circle only scales.

Visibility and locking

Both are properties of a level, not of a shape. Visibility gates whether a level's shapes are drawn in the plan, can be clicked, and get a mesh in the 3D view at all — which is also why a hidden level never reaches an OBJ, STL or image export, and never counts toward the GFA: there is no mesh for those to draw from. Locking stops a level's shapes from being selected, box-selected, moved or pushed and pulled, in the plan and in 3D; a locked shape still draws, still meshes, and still exports everywhere. DXF export is the one exception to all of this: it reads the shape list directly rather than the built meshes, and never checks visibility at all, so a hidden level's geometry still writes to a DXF file. So does "Save as .obj"'s embedded project, though not its geometry — see section 03.

Undo and redo

Every edit that calls a snapshot serialises the whole project state to a string and pushes it onto an undo stack, capped at 100 entries; undoing pops it back and parses it. A move, scale or point drag takes its snapshot on the first movement, so a plain click to select something leaves no step behind; a push/pull takes it the moment you press on a face. A large imported asset — an image's pixel data, an imported model's raw .obj text — is deliberately kept out of that state, in a separate lookup the source comments explains exists because the snapshot serialises everything on every edit, and an embedded image or model would otherwise be duplicated into every single undo step. Undo and redo never touch that lookup; removing an image or a model never deletes its data from it either. So undoing an import removes it from view and redoing it finds the same data still waiting, consistently, in both directions. The display options in the View menu live in the project state too, so undoing an edit also restores the display options as they were when that edit was made.

03

Import and export — what survives

This is the section with no counterpart in a tool that has methods to document instead of formats to move between. For each one: what a round trip keeps, and what it quietly drops.

DXF export

Every closed shape writes as a single LWPOLYLINE entity — not exploded into individual lines — and every circle writes as a CIRCLE. That choice is deliberate: a polyline re-imports as one connected shape rather than a scatter of disconnected segments, and it is what a real CAD package expects to read. A curve writes as its sampled outline, and each hole of a boolean result as a separate closed polyline. Dimensions and measured areas are not written.

DXF import

Reads exactly four entity types: LINE, CIRCLE, LWPOLYLINE, and the legacy POLYLINE/VERTEX/SEQEND triple. Anything else in the file — a block, a hatch, text, a dimension — is silently skipped with no count kept anywhere. The only signal the interface gives you is a warning if the file contained none of the four at all. Everything imported lands on the active level, with a height of zero.

OBJ and STL export

Both export the same set: every shape that is closed, has a height above zero, and is on a visible level — holes and base offsets included. An open reference line, or a footprint with no height yet, is excluded from either, and so is an imported reference model. Colour and opacity are not written. STL is written binary, with every vertex and normal stored as a 32-bit float; OBJ is written as plain text, full double precision, no rounding.

Save as .obj

Writes the same OBJ geometry as the export above, followed by the complete project — the same JSON as "Save", base64-encoded — on comment lines starting with #. The OBJ format defines comment lines as ignored by readers, so Rhino, Blender or SketchUp see ordinary geometry. Opening the file in this tool again finds that block and restores the project exactly, every level, curve, colour, dimension and imported asset included. Editing the file in another program and saving it from there will usually drop the comment block; it then opens as the "other programs" case below.

Opening an .obj

Two different things, from two different buttons. Import › .OBJ adds the file as a static reference, kept apart from the drawn shapes in its own group: it can be moved, scaled and deleted, but never edited as geometry, and it never exports again. Open, given an .obj with no embedded project, replaces the current project with a rebuilt one. Each object whose vertices all lie on exactly two heights, and whose side faces are all vertical, becomes an editable polygon: its footprint is traced from the unshared edges of its top faces (inner rings become holes) and its height is the gap between the two heights. Distinct base heights become levels — the first at or above zero is the Ground Floor, those below are basements. Any other object — a roof, a dome, a sloped face — comes in as one static reference model. The file is assumed to be Y-up and in metres; a Z-up file will not rebuild.

Image import

You are asked for one number: the image's real-world width, in metres. Height is never asked for — it is always the width you gave, multiplied by the image's own pixel aspect ratio, so the underlay can be resized without ever distorting.

Image export (PNG / JPEG)

Captures the 3D view only — the 2D plan is a separate canvas and is never captured — at 1×, 2× or 4× the view's on-screen size, or a custom width, keeping the view's proportions. The largest edge is capped by the graphics card (its maximum render-buffer and viewport size, and never above 16 384 pixels); the dialog shows the size you will actually get. Backgrounds: transparent (PNG only), white, or the on-screen dark grey. The selection highlight, a reference model's scale handle and any in-progress dimension are left out; dimensions and measured areas only if you tick that option. The "Translucent massing" style renders every volume at 55 % opacity with its edges drawn. Lines stay one pixel wide at any size, and labels are drawn from fixed-resolution textures, so both look thinner or softer at 4×. JPEG is saved at quality 0.95.

Save and open

A saved project is the entire state as JSON, with every imported image and model's raw data folded in alongside it. Opening it reverses that exactly. A project saved by version 1.0.0 opens with every new field at its default.

The round trip, plainly

Save then open, as .json or with "Save as .obj": everything comes back. Export DXF, re-import it: the same footprints come back, on one level, at no height — a curve returns as a polygon of its sampled points, and a courtyard returns as a separate filled shape rather than a hole. Export OBJ or STL: other programs read the geometry; opening that plain OBJ here rebuilds the straight extrusions as polygons, losing colour, curves and the circle/rectangle distinction.

05

How exact the numbers are

The arithmetic itself is exact for straight edges — there is no approximation, no solver converging on an answer, nothing to be near. The honest questions are about resolution and representation instead: what the grid costs you, where a curve becomes straight lines, what an export keeps, and what the tool actually guarantees.

What the grid costs

With Snap on, the smallest difference a point can express is one grid unit — one metre, by default — whether the point is being drawn, moved, scaled or edited. A move snaps the corner of the moving shape nearest to where you grabbed it, and carries the rest of the selection by the same amount, so a shape that started on the grid stays on it; a scale snaps the dragged corner and scales about the opposite one. A dimension's offset snaps to half a grid unit. The grid size itself has no fixed range in the source — any positive number can be typed into the toolbar's grid field. Reference images and the 2D image scale handles never snap.

Heights and the 3D drags

Heights follow different rules from plan positions. Dragging a top face, or Alt+dragging a volume up or down, first looks for another visible volume's top or bottom, or any level's elevation, within 12 screen pixels, and snaps there exactly. Failing that it rounds to 0.1 m with Snap on, or 0.01 m with it off — never to the grid size. Pushing a wall moves it by whole grid units with Snap on; a circle's radius snaps to whole grid units, never below one. A curve's side drag scales the whole outline about the average of its clicked points and does not snap at all.

Circles and curves are polygons in 3D

A circle's displayed area and perimeter are the exact πr² and 2πr. Its 3D volume, every OBJ and STL export, and any boolean result made from it use a 32-sided polygon instead — about 0.64 % less area and 0.16 % less perimeter. A curve has no exact form to compare against: its area, perimeter, volume and exports all come from the same 12 samples per span, so they agree with each other, and with nothing more precise.

Booleans

Each volume is treated as a straight prism: its footprint between a bottom and a top elevation. Intersect keeps the overlap of the footprints over the overlap of the two height ranges. Subtract keeps the first-selected volume's footprint minus the second's over its full height, plus the shared footprint above and below the second volume. Union merges the footprints when both volumes span exactly the same heights; otherwise it keeps the first volume whole and adds the pieces of the second that lie outside it. Results are new polygons on the first volume's level, in its colour and opacity; a circle or a curve becomes its sampled polygon and stays one. Slivers smaller than 0.0001 m² are discarded, and heights and base offsets are rounded to 0.1 mm.

Precision in the exports

DXF and OBJ both write coordinates at full double-precision JavaScript numbers, with no rounding applied anywhere in either exporter. STL is the one exception, and for a physical reason rather than a design choice: the binary STL format itself stores every vertex and normal as a 32-bit float, so re-importing an STL loses precision the DXF or OBJ export of the same model would not.

Where a wall push/pull can go wrong

The side-wall and radius drags carry three safety limits, and none of them is a correctness guarantee. The wall drag is rejected if it would shrink the shape's area below five per cent of what it started at — this catches an obvious collapse, but area alone cannot catch every way a polygon can fold in on itself; a drag that leaves the area roughly unchanged while crossing one of the shape's own non-adjacent edges is not guarded against. When the two edges neighbouring the one being dragged are near-parallel to it, the two corners the drag should reconstruct by intersecting lines cannot be found that way, and the code falls back to moving both corners in a straight line instead — a reasonable approximation, not the same geometric operation as the general case. The drag distance itself is separately clamped to five hundred units in either direction, a safety limit against a rare instability in the ground-plane arithmetic at a grazing camera angle, not a meaningful design constraint; a circle's radius is clamped between 0.05 and 1000 units by the same drag. A courtyard's own walls cannot be pushed at all.

What is actually asserted

The solid you see is the footprint you drew, extruded by its height from its base, and nothing more. There is no automated test suite anywhere in this application's source — no test files, no test script, nothing. What the tool computes has been read from the code for this page; it has not been checked against itself by any suite of its own.

07

Compared with SketchUp, BIM and a CAD package

Where it agrees

  • Push/pull. Dragging a shape's top face to change its height, or a side wall to move just that wall, is the same operation SketchUp's push/pull tool performs on a pushed face. The mechanics match; nothing here checks the result against anything.
  • Plan-to-solid workflow. Draw a footprint, give it a height, and you have a massing volume — the same first move a SketchUp or Rhino study starts with.
  • Booleans. Intersect, Union and Subtract are the same three operations a modelling package offers between solids — here restricted to straight vertical extrusions, which is what makes them exact.
  • Levels and elevations. Stacked floors at set elevations, read in an orthographic elevation, are how a CAD or BIM package presents a building's storeys.
  • DXF as a real interchange format. The DXF this tool writes and reads uses standard entity types a CAD package expects, closed as connected polylines rather than loose lines.

Where it diverges

  • No object model beyond a footprint and a height. A Revit wall carries a type, a thickness, layers, and relationships to the walls, floors and rooms around it. A Massing Studio "wall" is one side of an extruded footprint — geometry, a colour, and nothing else. There is no wall object here to compare.
  • Levels without storeys. A BIM level knows which elements belong to each storey and reports areas per floor accordingly. Here a level is an elevation with a name, and the area figures are sums of footprints — trap 1.
  • No BIM metadata of any kind. No IFC classes, no element properties, no schedules, no quantities beyond the geometric ones in section 01.
  • No analysis. Nothing here computes a structural, thermal, daylight or cost quantity — trap 3 again. A BIM tool with an analysis link, or a dedicated analysis package, is what that question needs.
  • No history or parametric relationships. A boolean result forgets its inputs, and moving one shape changes nothing else in the model, because nothing here knows the shapes are related at all.

The honest place for this tool: a fast way to test a footprint and a few storeys against each other before opening something heavier. Move to SketchUp, Rhino or a BIM package the moment you need materials, assemblies, or an answer that has to be checked rather than sketched.

08

Three exercises

Each isolates one variable and holds everything else fixed. Read the model, not the picture.

Exercise 01

Footprint depth governs how much of a room is daylit

  1. Draw a single rectangle 6 m wide. Set its height to one storey.
  2. Extend its depth from 6 m to 18 m in three steps, pushing one side wall each time — the tool's own push/pull, applied to one wall only. Check each depth with the Dimension tool.
  3. At each depth, mark by eye how far daylight from the 6 m face is likely to reach into the plan — roughly twice the window head height is the usual rule of thumb, applied here entirely outside the tool.
  4. Note the depth beyond which the far end of the room falls outside that reach.

Principle. The tool has no daylight model of any kind — trap 3 and the "Not modelled" ledger both say so. What it gives you is an exact footprint to measure a rule of thumb against by hand, which is a different and more honest use of the tool than expecting it to answer the question itself.

Exercise 02

The same floor area takes very different forms once storeys change

  1. Draw a footprint and extrude it to one storey. Compute its area by hand, using the shoelace formula this page names in section 01, and check your answer against the footprint area in the Properties panel.
  2. Add three levels. Draw a second footprint at a quarter of the plan area and stack it four storeys high with "Stack copy on top" — one shape per storey.
  3. Compare the two massing volumes in the 3D view and in the Front elevation. Their total floor area is the same by construction; read the Levels panel to confirm it, and note that their footprint and massing are not.
  4. Now draw a third option as a single shape, half the footprint area and two storeys tall. Note what the status bar's GFA says about it — and why.

Principle. The tool shows a footprint area, not a floor area. Step 4 is trap 1 in action: a two-storey volume drawn as one shape counts once. Checking the tool's number against your own arithmetic, and knowing what it sums, is the habit this exercise builds.

Exercise 03

A massing exported as .obj becomes another tool's input

  1. Build a simple massing study — a footprint or two, stacked over a few levels.
  2. Save it with "Save as .obj" (section 03 above covers exactly what travels with it: the geometry for other programs, and the whole project for this one).
  3. Import that .obj into Solar Analysis Lab or 3D Wind Rose, both of which read the same format.
  4. Note what each tool then does with the geometry that Massing Studio itself has no way to compute.

Principle. This is the strongest argument for where Massing Studio sits in the set: it is not the tool that answers a solar or wind question, it is the tool that produces the geometry those questions are asked of. The .obj export exists for exactly this handoff.

A caution for all three: read the model, and know what each number the tool gives you actually adds up. Where a figure matters, check it yourself and say how.

Everything on this page was read from the tool's source code. Where a claim could be checked against the code directly, it was; where the code did not make something clear, this page says so rather than inferring.

For anything beyond an early massing study, use the appropriate analysis tool. This tool is built to teach the relationship between a plan and a volume, and it contains no analysis of any kind — deliberately, so that what it does do stays legible.
